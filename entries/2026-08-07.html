Track A1 — Build the ontology (.ttl) from 소재 연구데이터 표준어휘 : 2025-2

Step 0: recover a structured term list from the PDF — current blocker

I have the complete 2025-2 vocabulary, but only as a PDF. That creates two problems: one routine, and one I want the professor's input on, because it decides the shape of everything downstream.

Problem 1 — extraction (routine, ~3 days)

  • First check: does the PDF have a real text layer, or is it scanned images? pdffonts / pdftotext answers this in minutes and decides whether this is a parsing job or an OCR job.
  • If born-digital: pdfplumber while retaining character coordinates (this matters for Problem 2); camelot or tabula if the terms sit in ruled tables.
  • If scanned: Korean OCR (PaddleOCR, or Tesseract with kor), then a second pass using the extracted term list itself as a correction lexicon — domain terms are exactly what generic OCR gets wrong.
  • Validation: compare the recovered term count against the count the document states in its own front matter, and hand-check a random sample of ~30 entries against the PDF. I will not proceed on an unvalidated extraction.
  • Output: one row per term carrying whatever fields the document actually provides — term ID, 국문 표제어, English label, definition, unit, synonyms, source note, deprecation status.

Problem 2 — the 분류체계, which is what I am actually stuck on

My difficulty is that the classification is not something I can read off the PDF as data. Before choosing a method I need to establish which of four situations we are in — this is answerable by inspection in an afternoon, but the four lead to completely different work, so I want to agree on the fallback in advance:

  1. Hierarchy is visual — carried by indentation, numbering (1 / 1.1 / 1.1.1), or heading levels. Recoverable, but only if extraction preserves x/y coordinates: cluster the left-edge x-positions into indent levels and read depth off the cluster. Plain text extraction destroys exactly this signal, which I suspect is why the structure currently looks unrecoverable to me.
  2. Hierarchy is encoded in the term ID — a notation scheme where the code prefix identifies the parent. Best case; parse the code and the tree falls out.
  3. Hierarchy lives only in the document structure — chapters and sections containing term tables. Reconstruct the path by tracking the current heading while walking pages in order.
  4. There is no hierarchy — a flat glossary with a 분야/category column, i.e. a faceted list rather than a tree. In that case skos:broader is simply the wrong construct and I should use skos:inScheme with a skos:Collection per facet.

What I do if the structure genuinely is not in the document

The governing principle: never invent hierarchy and attribute it to the standard. The fallback ladder:

  1. Ship v0.1 flat. SKOS explicitly permits a scheme with no broader relations at all. A flat, complete, correctly-labelled vocabulary is lossless, honest, and immediately usable — and it unblocks every other track.
  2. Add hierarchy as a separate overlay file (mat-voc-hierarchy.ttl), never merged into the vocabulary file, with our inferred links explicitly marked as ours. Anyone can then take the standard without our interpretation of it.
  3. Candidate sources for that overlay, cheapest first: term-ID codes; document chapter structure; Korean lexical head-matching (compound nouns are head-final, so a term ending in 강 is a strong candidate for skos:broader 강) — crude but surprisingly effective as a first pass; then LLM-proposed isA links with self-consistency voting, i.e. the OntoGen method; then alignment to EMMO/PMDco to inherit their upper structure.
  4. Expert validation on a sample before anything is promoted into a released file, with inter-annotator agreement recorded rather than assumed.

Why this blocker is actually an opportunity

Inducing a taxonomy when none is machine-readable is precisely the problem OntoGen was built for. Both outcomes are good for us:

  • If the PDF does contain the official 분류체계 (cases 1–3), then once extracted it becomes ground truth — and running an OntoGen-style pipeline against the term list gives a reconstruction benchmark directly parallel to the paper's ElementKG experiment (term accuracy, hierarchical F1, leaf-node accuracy). As far as I know, no Korean-language materials benchmark of that kind exists.
  • If it does not (case 4), then taxonomy induction is a genuine contribution rather than a preprocessing chore.

Either way the PDF constraint produces the evaluation setup instead of blocking it. That reframing is the main thing I want to check with the professor.

Two things I still need

  • The source file behind the PDF. If the committee holds an XLSX or a database export, one email removes Problem 1 entirely and probably Problem 2 as well. Highest-leverage item on this whole page — does the lab have a channel to ask?
  • Licensing and redistribution terms, since I intend to publish the .ttl openly.

Step 1: competency questions first, not classes first

I want to write ~30 competency questions before touching Protégé — the standard NeOn practice, and they double as the acceptance test suite for the KG later (each becomes a SPARQL query). Examples in our domain:

  • "Which samples of composition X were characterized by XRD and show property Y above threshold Z?"
  • "Which synthesis routes have been applied to material class C, and by which instrument?"
  • "What measurements exist for property P, and what are their units and uncertainty?"

If a term in the 표준어휘 is never needed by any competency question, it goes into the vocabulary layer but not into the reasoning layer. This keeps the OWL T-box small enough to stay decidable and maintainable.

Step 2: two-layer architecture (my proposal)

Rather than forcing every standard term to become an OWL class, I propose a deliberate split:

  • Layer 1 — SKOS vocabulary layer. The entire 표준어휘 published 1:1 as a skos:ConceptScheme: every term is a skos:Concept with skos:prefLabel@ko/@en, skos:definition, skos:broader, skos:altLabel for synonyms, and skos:notation carrying the official term ID. This is lossless and preserves the standard's authority — nothing is reinterpreted.
  • Layer 2 — OWL domain layer. A deliberately small T-box (~15–25 classes) covering the entities we actually reason over: Material, Sample, Process, Characterization, Property, Measurement, Instrument, Condition, Dataset, plus object properties with declared domain/range and disjointness axioms.
  • The bridge. Each OWL class carries rdfs:seeAlso / skos:exactMatch to its 표준어휘 concept. This is where OWL 2 punning earns its keep — see the open question below.

The payoff: the standard stays intact and citable, while the reasoning layer stays small. Reviewers of a future paper can check either layer independently.

Step 3: reuse before inventing

I want to align with existing materials ontologies rather than build in isolation — this is what makes the result publishable rather than a local data-cleaning exercise. Candidates to evaluate for alignment:

  • EMMO (Elementary Multiperspective Material Ontology) — the de-facto European upper ontology for materials.
  • PMDco (Platform MaterialDigital Core Ontology) and the NFDI-MatWerk ontologies — closest in intent to what we are doing, i.e. wrapping a national materials-data standard.
  • MDO (Materials Design Ontology) — for computational / DFT-derived data specifically.
  • CHAMEO — characterization methodology.
  • QUDT for units and quantity kinds, PROV-O for provenance, DCAT for dataset-level metadata.

Decision needed: is alignment to EMMO a requirement (adds rigor, costs ~3 weeks) or a nice-to-have for a later version?

Step 4: engineering and QC

  • Converter: Python + rdflib, XLSX → Turtle, deterministic and re-runnable, so a 2026-1 release regenerates cleanly.
  • IRI policy: stable base URI, readable English local names, Korean carried in labels not IRIs. owl:versionIRI mirroring the 2025-2 release tag.
  • Validation: pySHACL shapes, ELK/HermiT consistency check, OOPS! pitfall scan, ROBOT for release packaging, WIDOCO for HTML docs.
  • Everything in git, ontology docs published to GitHub Pages alongside this log.

Deliverable: mat-voc-2025-2.ttl (full SKOS) + mat-onto-0.1.ttl (OWL T-box, one pilot 분류 branch), by end of week 4.

Track A2 — Populate the knowledge graph

The ontology is the schema; the KG is the data. The most important open item here is where the instance data comes from, and that is a question for the professor.

Candidate data sources, in the order I would try them

  • Lab-internal records — our own experiments and DFT calculations. Highest value (nobody else has it), hardest to normalize.
  • Public databases — Materials Project, OQMD, AFLOW, NOMAD via their APIs. Immediate volume, good for testing the pipeline at scale before real data arrives.
  • Literature extraction — the OntoGen-style LLM pipeline, run against our own corpus. This is the part that reuses the paper directly.

My recommendation: build the pipeline against public data first so it is debugged by the time lab data is ready, then swap the source.

Non-negotiable design constraints

  • Provenance on every assertion. Every triple traceable via PROV-O to a DOI, an instrument run, or a calculation ID. Without this a research KG is not defensible — and it is also what makes the RAG answers citable in step A3.
  • Unit normalization at ingest via QUDT. Mixed units are the single most common failure mode in materials KGs.
  • Explicit entity resolution — a documented rule for when two records denote the same material, with owl:sameAs only where we are confident.

Storage decision to make at the meeting

  • RDF triplestore (Apache Jena Fuseki, or GraphDB Free) — native SPARQL 1.1, OWL reasoning, standards-compliant, exports cleanly.
  • Neo4j (property graph) — better tooling for GraphRAG and graph algorithms, but loses formal semantics.

My recommendation: RDF store as the single source of truth, with a scripted projection into Neo4j when GraphRAG needs it. We keep the semantics and still get the tooling; the cost is one export script.

Track A3 — LLM over the KG for discovery

This is where the work becomes a result rather than an infrastructure project. Two distinct goals, which I want to keep separate:

Goal 1: grounded retrieval (near-term, ~6 weeks)

Four approaches to implement and compare on the same corpus:

  • Baseline: plain vector RAG over the source text. No graph.
  • Schema-aware text-to-SPARQL: the T-box is placed in the prompt, the LLM emits SPARQL, we execute it. This directly exploits our ontology and gives exact, verifiable answers with provenance.
  • GraphRAG (community-summary style, local and global search) over the KG projection.
  • Hybrid: vector search over skos:definition for entity linking, then structured traversal from the linked entities.

My prior is that hybrid wins, and that the interesting result is how much the ontology layer contributes — which is an ablation, not a demo.

Goal 2: actual discovery (the paper story)

Retrieval alone is a tool paper. The stronger story closes the loop with the lab's existing computational strength:

  1. Run link prediction / missing-edge detection over the populated KG to surface material–property combinations that should exist but are unreported.
  2. Have the LLM rank and rationalize those candidates against the literature in the graph.
  3. Validate the top candidates with DFT. This is the step that turns a graph artifact into a materials-science claim, and it is exactly where Prof. Jang's group has the capability.

Even a small number of validated predictions makes this a Digital Discovery / npj Computational Materials submission rather than a workshop paper.

Evaluation

  • Gold set of ~50 competency questions with expert-verified answers (reuses the Step 1 output — no extra work).
  • Metrics: execution accuracy, answer correctness, hallucination rate, and citation validity (does every claim resolve to a real provenance node?).
  • Ablations: with/without ontology; SKOS-only vs full OWL T-box; and the A-box/T-box variant described in the open question below.

Serving note: all models run locally on the DGX via vLLM. Unpublished lab data never leaves the lab network. This is the strongest argument for Track B, and I will present it that way.

Open research question — are A-box and T-box interchangeable?

Where the confusion comes from

Reading Oarga et al., Digital Discovery 2026, 5, 1269–1279 (OntoGen), two things in the paper make the boundary look fluid:

  • Definition 2.1 defines an ontology as a conceptualization {C, R, I} — with the set of instances I included inside the ontology. Under the classical description-logic reading, instances are the A-box and sit outside the terminology.
  • Section 2.2.4 (KG instantiation) assigns class-vs-instance status by position in the graph: "the leaf nodes of the taxonomy are considered to be instances for the KG … Conversely, non-leaf nodes, having children related through isA relationships, are considered classes."

The second point is what actually caused my confusion, and I think the confusion is justified: under that rule, a node's status is not intrinsic. Their taxonomy is built incrementally across papers, so a term that is a leaf (and therefore an A-box individual) after iteration 3 can acquire a child at iteration 7 and silently become a T-box class. The same term changes ontological category because of what else happened to be extracted. That genuinely looks like interchangeability.

My current position, which I want challenged

  1. Formally, no — they are not interchangeable. In OWL DL the T-box holds terminological axioms (rdfs:subClassOf, owl:equivalentClass, domain/range, disjointness) and the A-box holds assertions about individuals (rdf:type, property assertions, owl:sameAs). They support different inference: subsumption and classification vs. instance retrieval and consistency. The concrete asymmetry that bites us: rdfs:subClassOf is transitive, rdf:type is not. If I model "austenitic stainless steel" as an individual of Steel, then a query for "all subtypes of Steel" will not reach anything below it. Swap the modeling and the query results change. That is a real, testable difference, not a philosophical one.
  2. But there are three legitimate ways the boundary moves, which explains why the paper's usage is not simply wrong:
    • Punning (OWL 2 DL). The same IRI may be used as both a class and an individual; the reasoner treats them as two distinct semantic objects that share a name, and decidability is preserved. This is exactly how I plan to bridge the two layers in A1 — "Steel" is a class of samples and an individual concept in the 표준어휘 with a term ID.
    • Metaclasses (OWL Full / RDFS). Genuinely treats classes as individuals, and is undecidable. Avoid.
    • SKOS. Sidesteps the question entirely — every term is an individual of skos:Concept linked by skos:broader, and no class/instance judgment is ever required. Note that skos:broader is deliberately not transitive (that is skos:broaderTransitive), precisely so vocabularies avoid committing to subsumption.
  3. The criterion I propose for our project: a term belongs in the T-box if we will ever assert individuals of it; it belongs in the A-box if it is itself a thing in the world with its own identity — this sample, this instrument, this measurement run, this dataset. Applied to the 표준어휘, that puts essentially the entire standard vocabulary in the T-box/SKOS layer, and sources the A-box from lab data. Which means the OntoGen leaf-node heuristic would be wrong for our use case: a leaf like "austenitic stainless steel" is a type, not an individual.
  4. Resolution. I do not think this is an error in the paper so much as a scope statement. The authors say so themselves, immediately after Definition 2.1: their definition "represents a computational approximation to formal ontological frameworks rather than a complete logical ontology," reflecting "the practical constraints of automated extraction from natural language." The leaf-node rule is an artifact of needing some automatable criterion, not an ontological claim. It is defensible for zero-shot extraction and not defensible as a modeling principle for a curated standard.

Turning the confusion into an experiment

The question I actually want to ask the professor: does the choice measurably matter downstream? We are in an unusually good position to answer it, because A3 already has an evaluation harness. Same corpus, same questions, three encodings:

  • (a) SKOS-flat — everything a concept, no OWL semantics.
  • (b) Deep OWL T-box + thin A-box — our proposed design.
  • (c) OntoGen-style leaf-as-instance.

Then measure retrieval and answer quality on the competency-question set. If (b) wins, the ontology engineering effort is justified with evidence rather than assertion. If (a) or (c) is within noise, that is a genuinely interesting negative result about how much formal semantics LLM-based retrieval actually exploits — and worth reporting either way.

This is the item I most want the professor's reaction to, because it determines whether the ontology work is a means or an end.
