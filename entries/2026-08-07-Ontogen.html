Why TBox and ABox Are Interchangeable in OntoGen

2026-08-07

Subject of analysis: A. Oarga, M. Hart, A. M. Bran, M. Lederbauer and P. Schwaller, "Scientific knowledge graph and ontology generation using open large language models," Digital Discovery, 2026, 5, 1269–1279. DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00275c. Published under CC-BY 4.0; quotations below are attributed by section and page.

Summary of the argument

In OntoGen, whether a term belongs to the TBox (a class) or the ABox (an instance) is determined by whether it is a leaf node in the taxonomy, and leaf status changes as the incremental algorithm iterates. Combined with the fact that the pipeline extracts only a single edge type (isA) and adopts no upper-level ontology, instanceOf is effectively a post-hoc relabelling of isA at the leaf boundary. The TBox/ABox line is therefore not an ontological boundary but a topological one — movable, and in both directions.

1. The criterion is leaf vs. non-leaf, not the nature of the concept

The rule is stated in §2.2.4, Knowledge graph instantiation (p. 1272), and it is purely structural:

"the leaf nodes of the taxonomy are considered to be instances for the KG"

The justification appeals only to graph position:

"since leaf nodes have no children, they should be treated as the lowest-level concepts"

And the converse:

"nonleaf nodes, having children related through isA relationships, are considered classes in the ontological format rather than instances for the KG"

Point: no step in the pipeline asks whether a term denotes a universal or a particular. "Has children or not" is the entire classifier.

2. instanceOf is a relabelling of isA, not an independently extracted relation

§2.2.3 (p. 1272) states that the taxonomy carries exactly one relation type:

"The relationships in our extracted taxonomic structures consist of isA relationships in the form of {(c_i, r, c_j) | c_i, c_j ∈ C and r = isA}"

The extraction function is explicitly agnostic as to which side of the boundary its output lands on — the authors say so directly in the same section:

"query_relationships extracts pairs (s,t) of isA relationships ... where s is a term present in the taxonomy T(k) and t is a term from the vocabulary V_i which will either represent a class or an instance in the ontological schema"

Point: the LLM is asked a single question ("is A a kind of B?") and returns untyped edges. The instanceOf edges shown in Fig. 1 (p. 1270) are not separately extracted; they are isA edges renamed once the leaf rule of §2.2.4 is applied.

In description logic, subsumption (C ⊑ D) and assertion (D(a)) are distinct kinds of statement — the first transitive, the second not. Collapsing them into one edge type removes the distinction at the syntactic level, before any semantics is assigned.

3. Leaf status drifts across iterations, so the assignment is unstable

Algorithm 1 (p. 1272) is incremental, and the authors explain why multiple passes are required:

"This procedure builds a taxonomy incrementally, requiring multiple iterations since a term may not be placed in the earlier taxonomies but may find its position in a later one"

A term that is a leaf at iteration k (hence an ABox instance) becomes a non-leaf at iteration k+1 (hence a TBox class) as soon as anything is attached beneath it. This is not hypothetical; the paper's own descriptive statistics document it (§3.1, p. 1274):

"The figures illustrate how the number of isA relationships increases constantly over iterations"

In Fig. 4 (p. 1274) and Fig. 5 (p. 1276), the #nodes curve grows persistently faster than #leaves, which means former leaves are being internalised at scale.

Point: TBox/ABox membership is a function of the iteration count K. The same corpus and the same code, stopped at a different iteration, yield a different class/instance partition.

4. No upper-level ontology supplies a universal/particular criterion

This is the root cause. In §2.1 (p. 1270) the authors deliberately forgo foundational ontological commitments:

"our approach does not employ formal upper-level ontologies that provide foundational ontological commitments"

"we extract domain-specific categories directly from the literature, which results in a more practical but less formally grounded taxonomic structure"

The qualification attached to Definition 2.1 is equally explicit:

"This definition represents a computational approximation to formal ontological frameworks rather than a complete logical ontology"

Point: the TBox/ABox distinction rests on the distinction between universals and particulars, and that distinction can only be supplied axiomatically by an upper-level ontology (BFO, DOLCE, EMMO, and similar). Without one, nothing adjudicates whether Aluminia in Fig. 1 (p. 1270) should be an instance of Support or a subclass of it. The paper chooses the former:

"The ontology has three isA and is related to the KG through three instance of relationships"

Yet alumina plainly admits subclasses of its own (α-Al₂O₃, γ-Al₂O₃), so it could sit on the TBox side with equal legitimacy. Both placements are consistent with everything the pipeline knows.

5. The downstream task flattens the two again, which is why it costs the authors nothing

The enhanced configuration in §3.2.2 (p. 1276) merges the isA edges straight into the triple graph:

"we extend the KG by adding isA relationships. The only difference between (1) and (2) is that the second approach incorporates isA relationships"

In the GraphRAG pipeline that follows, all nodes are treated homogeneously; isA contributes nothing but additional connectivity for community detection and retrieval.

Point: the collapsed boundary produces no observable loss on this particular task — the win rates in Table 5 (p. 1276) remain decisive. The cost is paid elsewhere: the resulting artefact cannot support DL reasoning, consistency checking, or alignment with existing ontologies. It is a labelled graph that improves retrieval, not an ontology in the description-logic sense, and the paper is careful never to claim otherwise.

6. The symptoms reported in expert evaluation are consistent with this root cause

The two observations recorded in §3.2.3 (p. 1277) are characteristic of a taxonomy with no upper-level anchoring:

"some extracted vocabulary terms are heavily context-dependent and may lose their intended meaning when placed in a more general context"

"the taxonomy contains instances of semantically similar concepts repeated in different parts of the structure"

The concrete failure cases the authors give point the same way:

"(e.g., Matrix isA Support Material)"

"Electrocatalytic Activity isA Catalytic Performance and Activity isA Catalytic Performance, rather than Electrocatalytic Activity isA Activity"

Overall correctness, as judged by two domain experts:

"on average, 64.5% of the examined relationships were deemed correct by the experts"

Point: the second example shows that even hierarchy depth within the TBox is unconstrained — Electrocatalytic Activity should attach under Activity but is instead flattened directly under Catalytic Performance. The looseness of the class/instance boundary and the looseness of the hierarchy itself have the same origin: no axioms constrain either.

Appendix: the circularity in the formal interface

Definition 2.2 (§2.1, p. 1270) is the only formal bridge between the ontology and the KG:

"S is the set of subjects where s ∈ S is an instance of a concept from the ontology ⇒ S ∈ I"

The definition requires subjects to be instances of ontology concepts, but membership in I (the instance set) is itself conferred by the leaf rule of §2.2.4. The definition is circular at this point: instances are defined as leaf nodes, and leaf nodes are defined as instances. No criterion for instancehood independent of graph shape is ever supplied.

Contrast: why constrained pipelines do not exhibit this

Extraction pipelines built on pre-existing formal ontologies (EMMO, CMSO/ASMO, MDS-Onto, and similar) fix the TBox in advance, with axioms attached. The LLM's task is narrowed to populating the ABox with individuals and binding them to IRIs. Class/instance status is conferred by the ontology, not inferred from the shape of the extraction output.

The cost is a marked loss of extraction freedom — new classes outside the ontology's coverage cannot be discovered. The benefit is precisely what OntoGen gives up: TBox and ABox are not interchangeable, and therefore SHACL validation, DL reasoning, and cross-ontology alignment become meaningful operations rather than vacuous ones.

If OntoGen is cited as related work, points 1, 2 and 4 above form the most compact statement of the limitation, and all three rest on the authors' own text rather than on external argument.
